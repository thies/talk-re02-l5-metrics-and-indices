Measuring performance
What is the performance of a real estate investment?

  • What does it earn?
    • What rent does the building collect, and what is left after vacancy, running costs and repairs?
  • Return to whom?
    • Should we borrow half the price, and what does that do to our return and our risk?
  • Measured how?
    • Over a five-year hold, do the projected cash flows beat the return we require?
  • Compared with what?
    • What yield does the asking price imply, and what happens to value if yields move?
    • Would a property index tell us whether we did well?

From rent roll to NOI
What income does the building collect, and what is left after running it?

  • Potential gross income in Year 1 is the passing rent: £350,000
  • Less vacancy and collection loss of 5% (£17,500): effective gross income of £332,500
  • Less operating expenditure at 20% of effective income (£66,500): Year 1 NOI of £266,000
  • NOI measures property operations before financing costs and income tax

Operating expenditure here means costs the landlord cannot recover from tenants.

NOI is not cash in the investor’s pocket
Capital spending comes out next

  • Year 1 NOI: £266,000
  • Capital expenditure: assumed £40,000 a year for an older building
  • Property-before-tax cash flow: £266,000 − £40,000 = £226,000
  • Debt service, tax, purchase costs and sale costs sit elsewhere in the cash-flow model

Two components of property return
Capital growth plus income return equals total return

  • Capital growth (capital gain, appreciation return): change in asset value, relative to opening value
    • A price index alone omits income. A property decision needs the whole cash-flow picture.
  • Income return: net income received, relative to opening value
  • Total return: the sum of the two, not a third source
  • MSCI reports exactly these components for the market, so our building’s return can sit beside an index return

Year 1 return on our office
What did the investor earn, before and after inflation?

  • Assume the value rises from £3.00m to £3.03m and inflation \(\pi\) is 2% (illustrative)
  • Capital growth: \(£30{,}000\div£3.0\text{m}=\) 1.00%; income return: \(£226{,}000\div£3.0\text{m}=\) 7.53%
  • Nominal total return \(r\): 1.00% + 7.53% = 8.53%
  • Real total return: \((1+r)\div(1+\pi)-1=1.0853\div1.02-1=\) 6.40%; the shortcut \(r-\pi\) gives 6.53%
  • Inflation mostly hits capital growth: real growth here is about −1%, while the income return changes less

Income after capital expenditure; value and inflation are teaching assumptions.

Yields and required returns
What does a 9.5% cap rate imply about value and returns?

  • Direct capitalisation: \(\text{Value}=\text{Annual NOI}\div\text{Cap rate}\). As in Week 2, a cap-rate shock moves value before rent can respond.

  • Required return: Week 3 built it as \(r=r_f+RP\); from the income side, \(r=\text{cash yield}+g\)
  • Ours after capex: \(\small (£285{,}000-£40{,}000)\div£3.0\text{m}=8.17\%\); plus 1.5% growth implies \(r\approx\) 9.67%
    • The 9.50% cap rate on NOI overstates the cash return: capital spending is a real cost, larger for older buildings
  • The spread over a reference rate pays for risk, liquidity and growth. A low cap rate may reflect low risk or high expected growth; the ratio alone cannot say which.

Cash-on-cash and leverage
What does equity receive, and at what risk?

  • Year 1 cash flow to equity: £266,000 NOI − £40,000 capex − £97,500 interest = £128,500
  • \(\text{Cash-on-cash return}=\frac{\text{Year 1 cash flow to equity}}{\text{Equity invested}}=£128{,}500\div£1{,}500{,}000=\) 8.57%; a one-period cash yield, not an IRR
  • The property’s 7.53% cash yield exceeds the 6.5% loan rate, so equity earns 8.57%: positive leverage
  • When NOI or value falls, interest and principal remain, so equity risk rises
  • In 2023–24 many prime deals had cap rates below the loan rate: negative leverage. Why would a buyer accept that?

NPV, IRR and equity multiple
Does the forecast clear the hurdle?

  • NPV (Week 3’s DCF, less the price paid): \(\text{NPV}=\sum_{t=0}^{T}\frac{\text{CF}_t}{(1+r)^t}\); about −£50,000 at a 12% equity target, so the forecast falls just short
  • IRR: the discount rate that sets NPV to zero; property 8.84%, equity 11.11%. It reflects timing but can mislead with unusual cash-flow patterns.
  • Equity multiple: \(\frac{\text{Total cash received}}{\text{Equity invested}}\); about 1.58×. It ignores timing.
  • 12% is the investor’s target. The market’s required return on this leveraged equity is higher:
    • \(r_E=6.5\%+2\times(9.67\%-6.5\%)=\) 12.8%

Do we walk away?
Forecast unleveraged IRR of 8.84%, required return of 9.67%

  • The forecast falls short of what the market requires for this risk. Do we walk away?
  • What would you offer instead?
  • What happens once we pay the 6.8% purchase costs?
  • Could anyone rationally pay the asking price?

Do we walk away? Answer
A verdict on the price, not the building

  • Discounted at 9.67%, the forecast cash flows are worth about £2.90m: offer that or less, or walk away
  • Purchase costs cut the unleveraged IRR to about 7.2%; the break-even price falls to about £2.72m plus costs
  • Without costs the gap is small: a 9.0% exit yield lifts the IRR to 9.78%, above the required return
  • Investment value can exceed market value: lower funding costs, better management or a refurbishment plan
  • Without such an edge, paying above market value only transfers wealth to the seller

IRR versus time-weighted return
Why can a deal return and an index return disagree?

  • TWR: compounds periodic returns, so money invested does not matter
    • TWR \(=[(1+r_1)\cdots(1+r_T)]^{1/T}-1\)
  • IRR: money-weighted, so periods with more capital count for more
  • Example: £1m earns +20%, a further £1m is added, and the £2.2m then earns −10%
    • TWR: \(\sqrt{1.20\times0.90}-1=\) +3.92%; IRR: −0.67%
  • Use TWR for indices and managers without control of capital flows; use IRR for a single property or where the manager controls timing

Why property needs indices
How to track performance without a central market?

  • Total return needs prices, rents, taxes and costs, and none of them is readily observable
  • Many financial securities trade on a central market that produces prices almost 24/7
  • Real estate is traded privately, and assets are unique in location and quality
  • Three general approaches: our own valuations, information from transactions, or information from financial markets
  • Past returns matter, for example for manager compensation and taxation, but we mostly care about the future: do the fundamentals remain in place?

News-response time
By index type

  • Stock market-based indices react fastest (as efficient as the REIT market itself)
  • Transaction-based indices lag: deals take months to close
  • Valuation-based indices lag most: months, sometimes quarters

MSCI (2024).

IPD/MSCI office
Valuation-based indices are too smooth: SD 1.19%, FOAC 0.86

  • Recap, Week 3: appraisals are infrequent and update partially, so index returns are smoothed and autocorrelated
  • Can we fix this at the asset level? Not really: appraisers have good reasons to incorporate new information gradually.
  • We can, and should, alleviate the problem at the index level.

De-smoothing
A band-aid for valuation-based indices

  • Model the smoothed return as a weighted average of the previous return and the “true” return: \(r_t=\alpha r_{t-1}+(1-\alpha)r_t^\ast\)
  • Rearrange and solve for the true return: \(r_t^\ast=\frac{r_t-\alpha r_{t-1}}{1-\alpha}\)
  • De-smoothed with \(\alpha=0.7\): mean unchanged, SD 1.19% → 2.1%, FOAC 0.86 → 0.13

Are transactions more timely?
Transaction-based indices lag true trends as well

  • They lag less than appraisals, but they still lag
  • Closing a property deal often takes months: agree a price in June, exchange contracts in August?
  • Very few transactions happen in market downturns
    • Financial constraints and loss aversion keep owners from selling below a certain price (see, e.g., Genesove and Mayer, 2001)

Space-time dilemma
Frequency of index or geographic and sector detail?

  • Pick your poison: high frequency needs broad coverage; narrow coverage forces lower frequency.

Is the benchmark representative?
Does the Gherkin feature in any UK index?

  • EPRA (stock market-based): REITs must meet minimum free-float market cap and liquidity criteria
  • MSCI/IPD (valuation-based): properties from contributing organisations only. What about sovereign wealth funds and overseas investors?
  • Transaction-based: how often are landmark buildings sold? Not since 2014.

Rents and costs
Total return: price appreciation + yield − costs and taxes

  • Data on rents and costs are even harder to obtain than sale prices: there is no land registry for short-term rents
  • The challenges resemble those for price indices: appraisals, hedonic analysis or repeat-rent indices?
  • Rents and prices are often observed in different samples, which is a problem if properties or locations are not comparable

More: Eichholtz, Korevaar, Lindenthal & Tallec (2021).

A good investment?
An easy question, really hard to answer!

  • Exceptional performance, low risk, high return?
    • According to Jordà et al. (2019) residential real estate offers high returns and low risk: they speak of a “housing risk premium puzzle”.
  • With better data and more careful estimation, the picture gets less rosy
    • Eichholtz et al. (2021) and Chambers et al. (2021) show Sharpe ratios are significantly lower when measuring returns at asset level.

Index accuracy matters
Asset-level total returns versus combined rent, price and cost indices

  • Real net total returns: 4.0% (Paris), 4.8% (Amsterdam), entirely from rental yields
  • Lower and less risky than Jordà et al. (2019) for the same cities: yields imputed from price indices are prone to error

Risk
(Good) indices capture systematic risk, only

  • Risk at single-asset level is the realistic case for most investors
  • Idiosyncratic risk (blue) dominates, especially at shorter holding periods

Summing up
From one building to the market

  • A metric is only as useful as its cash flows, definitions and assumptions
  • Perfect return data do not exist: know how your benchmark was collected and processed
  • Data availability determines the most suitable index methodology
  • Market returns are usually not available to individual investors: risk for one property exceeds risk for a city, sector or national index
  • Total returns matter: do not just look at capital gains
