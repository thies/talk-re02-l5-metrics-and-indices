Today’s question
Would you invest in this Cambridge office?

  • What income does it produce?
  • How does income translate into value?
  • What changes when the purchase is financed?
  • Do the projected cash flows justify the price?

Two levels of measurement
Market benchmarks and single assets

  • Market indices summarise changes across many properties or listed shares
  • Property analysis asks whether a particular asset’s cash flows justify its price and risk
  • The index recordings explain how benchmarks are constructed. Today we use that context to analyse one investment.

Two components of property return
Income return plus capital growth equals total return

  • Income return: net income received, relative to opening value
  • Capital growth: change in asset value, relative to opening value
  • Total return: the sum of the two, not a third source
  • A price index alone omits income. A property decision needs the whole cash-flow picture.

Geltner et al. (2026), Ch. 3; MSCI (2024).

How indices connect to property metrics
Using MSCI measures as market evidence

  • Income return tracks net income relative to capital value
  • Capital growth tracks change in property values, net of capital spending
  • Equivalent yield links expected rental cash flows to capital value

\(TR_t=\dfrac{CV_t-CV_{t-1}-CExp_t+CRpt_t+NI_t}{CV_{t-1}+CExp_t}\times100\)

\(Index_t=Index_{t-1}\left(1+\dfrac{TR_t}{100}\right),\quad Index_0=100\)

  • Use indices as market evidence; do not confuse a market benchmark with the forecast for one building

MSCI (2024). CV: capital value; CExp: capital expenditure; CRpt: capital receipts; NI: net income.

Our running example
An illustrative Cambridge office

  • All figures are teaching assumptions, not a valuation or sourced market evidence

From rent roll to effective income
What income does the building actually collect?

  • Potential gross income in Year 1 is the passing rent: £300,000
  • Vacancy and collection loss: 5% × £300,000 = £15,000
  • Effective gross income: £300,000 − £15,000 = £285,000
  • Question: should vacancy be applied to market rent, passing rent, or a tenant-by-tenant rent roll?

Geltner et al. (2026), Ch. 5.

Operating costs and NOI
What is left after running the building?

  • Assume operating expenditure is 25% of effective income
  • Operating expenditure: 25% × £285,000 = £71,250
  • Year 1 NOI: £285,000 − £71,250 = £213,750
  • NOI measures property operations before financing costs and income tax

Geltner et al. (2026), Ch. 5.

NOI is not cash in the investor’s pocket
Capital spending comes out next

  • Year 1 NOI: £213,750
  • Capital expenditure: assumed £25,000 a year
  • Property-before-tax cash flow: £213,750 − £25,000 = £188,750
  • Debt service, tax, purchase costs and sale costs sit elsewhere in the cash-flow model

Geltner et al. (2026), Ch. 5.

Check your understanding
What if vacancy doubles?

  • If vacancy rises from 5% to 10%, with all else unchanged, what happens to NOI?
  • Effective income = £300,000 × 90% = £270,000
  • Operating costs at 25% = £67,500; NOI = £202,500
  • A five percentage-point vacancy increase reduces NOI by £11,250 under these assumptions

Capitalisation rate: income relative to value
How is income priced relative to value?

Capitalisation rate = NOI ÷ property value

  • Year 1 passing-rent NOI: £213,750 ÷ £5.0m = 4.28%
  • At market rent after the review: £249,375 ÷ £5.0m = 4.99%
  • This is a market pricing ratio. It is not, by itself, a complete forecast of total return.

Geltner et al. (2026), Ch. 4–5.

Yield language depends on convention
Same word, different definitions

  • Net initial yield (UK): passing net income divided by price plus stated acquisition costs, under the chosen convention
  • Equivalent yield (UK): the discount rate that equates the value with current and future rental cash flows under lease assumptions
  • Reversionary yield: market rent relative to capital value, highlighting potential income at reversion
  • Cap rate (common US usage): typically current or stabilised NOI divided by value

Passing rent versus market rent
When does the reversion arrive?

  • Passing rent is £300,000; assumed market rent is £350,000
  • The gap does not arrive immediately if leases are fixed
  • Review dates, incentives, voids, tenant quality and costs affect the timing and certainty of reversion
  • Equivalent yield incorporates the timing of those future cash flows; a headline yield can conceal it

Yield spreads are a judgement
Compared with what, and for which risks?

  • A property yield is often compared with a government bond or another reference rate
  • The spread must compensate for differences in risk, liquidity, growth, lease structure and asset quality
  • There is no single “risk-free rate” that automatically answers the valuation question
  • Ask: which cash flows, which horizon and which risks does this yield imply?

Introduce debt
How is the purchase financed?

  • Assume 50% loan-to-value and an interest-only loan at 5.5%
  • Debt: 50% × £5.0m = £2.50m
  • Initial equity: £2.50m, before transaction costs
  • Annual interest: 5.5% × £2.50m = £137,500

Cash-on-cash return
What does equity receive in Year 1?

  • Year 1 cash flow to equity = NOI − capex − interest
  • £213,750 − £25,000 − £137,500 = £51,250
  • Cash-on-cash return = £51,250 ÷ £2,500,000 = 2.05%
  • This is a one-period cash yield on invested equity, not an IRR

What leverage changes
A smaller equity cheque, but more risk

  • Debt reduces the initial equity cheque and adds a contractual payment
  • When property performance exceeds the borrowing cost, leverage can raise equity returns
  • When NOI or value falls, interest and principal obligations remain; equity risk rises
  • Separate property-level performance from shareholder/equity performance

Geltner et al. (2026), Ch. 12.

Forecast the exit
What might the building sell for after Year 5?

  • Rent resets to £350,000 at the end of Year 1; NOI after that review is £249,375. It then grows by 2.5% annually.
  • A buyer at the end of Year 5 pays for the income ahead, so we capitalise Year 6 NOI

Year 6 NOI = £249,375 × 1.0254 ≈ £275,260

  • At a 5.25% exit yield, indicative sale value ≈ £5.24m
  • The exit yield is an assumption and can dominate the result. Annual growth is a simplification: UK leases usually hold rent fixed between reviews.

Geltner et al. (2026), Ch. 4–5.

From sale price to sale proceeds
What does equity receive on sale?

  • Indicative Year 5 value: £5.24m
  • Selling costs at 2%: about £105,000
  • Repay interest-only debt: £2.50m
  • Net sale proceeds to equity: about £2.64m
  • Excludes tax, transaction costs on purchase, lease-up costs and any debt fees

NPV: value at a chosen hurdle rate
Does the forecast clear the hurdle?

NPV = Σt=0T CFt ÷ (1 + r)t

  • At an 8% equity hurdle, the example NPV is approximately −£363,000
  • Negative NPV means this forecast falls short of the stated hurdle
  • Change the exit yield, rent growth or discount rate and inspect the sensitivity

Geltner et al. (2026), Ch. 4.

IRR and equity multiple
Two summaries of the same cash flows

  • IRR: discount rate that makes forecast NPV equal zero; this example gives about 4.46%
  • Equity multiple: total cash received ÷ equity invested; about 1.23× here
  • IRR reflects timing but can be misleading with unusual cash-flow patterns
  • The multiple ignores timing; neither metric explains risk on its own

IRR: Geltner et al. (2026), Ch. 3.

Metrics answer different questions
Match the metric to the question

  • NOI: how is the property operating?
  • Yield: how is income priced relative to value?
  • LTV, DSCR and debt yield: how much borrowing risk is present?
  • Cash-on-cash, NPV, IRR and equity multiple: what does the investment deliver to equity?

Investment judgement
A metric is only as useful as its cash flows, definitions and assumptions.

  • Read the lease
  • Check the costs
  • Stress the yield
  • Explain the risk
